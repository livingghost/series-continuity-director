# Weather, Light, Environment, and Image Quality

Atmosphere is a physical system. It changes bodies, materials, sound, visibility, or movement instead of replacing scene content with a mood label.

## 1. Environment as an acting system

Build atmosphere from linked evidence:

```text
far carrier
near carrier
body or object interaction
sound or light consequence
```

A sequence may distribute these carriers across shots, but the environment must remain one coherent state.

## 2. Weather carriers and body response

### Wind

- far: trees, flags, rain, smoke;
- near: paper, hems, hair, fur tips, dust;
- body: narrowed eyes, held collar, braced balance;
- sound: wind around a named structure or opening.

### Rain

- far: rain layers and cloud light;
- middle: roof, rail, umbrella, awning, or window impact;
- near: puddle, sleeve water, runoff, drops, splash;
- body: wet surfaces, changed grip, careful footing, sheltering gesture;
- sound: continuous bed plus a functional impact or change.

### Snow

- far: layered fall and reduced distant contrast;
- near: flakes landing on a visible surface;
- body: footprints, melting, breath when plausible, altered clothing;
- light: environmental fill that preserves local color.

### Heat and humidity

- environment: haze, still air, fan movement, condensation;
- body: sweat, flushing, breath, fatigue, changed pace or distance;
- wardrobe: loosened collar, rolled sleeves, carried outer layer;
- surface: damp hair, clumped fur, or fabric adhesion where exposure supports it.

## 3. Water and wet state

Record:

- source and direction;
- first contact point;
- darkening, clinging, flattening, or transparency pattern;
- drops, runoff, ripples, splash, wake, or condensation source;
- reflection and refraction;
- drying process when time passes.

Use state events or processes when wetness persists. A cut does not restore dry clothing or fur without elapsed time, shelter, drying, replacement, or another approved event.

## 4. Smoke, steam, dust, fog, and suspended material

Record source, density, depth, movement, light interaction, and occlusion.

- steam rises from hot water, food, breath, or a device;
- smoke follows fire, exhaust, incense, or a held object;
- dust follows footsteps, impact, collapse, or airflow;
- fog occupies environment depth;
- spray and particles follow a named source and path.

Keep identity, action, and contact readable. Local atmosphere should not become a global softness filter unless the visual language requires it.

## 5. Light source, landing, and continuity

For each important source, record:

```text
location
color and intensity relationship
direction
landing surfaces
shadow side
movement or flicker
effective story range
```

Name practical sources when possible. When a subject blocks, enters, or leaves light, treat that change as performance and state evidence. Camera changes may reveal different landing surfaces, but source position and scene logic remain coherent.

A hard low source puts a body on screen twice: as the body itself, and as the shadow it throws. The
cast shadow is a second image of the same pose, lying flat, and when the source is behind the subject
that shadow reaches toward the camera and is frequently the more readable of the two. Direct it as a
subject in its own right:

```text
what the shadow falls on
which way it points and how far it reaches
whose pose it repeats, and which parts of that pose survive in it
whether it touches or crosses anything else in frame
```

A backlit figure whose shadow is left unspecified will still cast one, and it will show whatever pose
the surface invents, which can contradict the figure it belongs to. If the shadow is not wanted in
frame, say where it goes instead.

## 6. Color stability in warm scenes

A warm practical source should read as a source, not as an unrestricted global grade. Two techniques help preserve color and material clarity when the approved style calls for balanced warmth.

### Dual reference temperature

Keep one cool or neutral reference visible when a warm source dominates, such as night beyond a window, an off-white wall, pale stone, glass, or neutral metal. The reference lets the viewer read the warmth as light landing from a source. Without it, local illumination can collapse into a uniform warm cast.

Record both the warm source and the neutral or cool reference, including where each remains visible across coverage.

### Specular anchors

Name one or two clean reflective edges, such as a glass rim, metal handle, polished fitting, wet rail, or instrument edge. Use them as local sharpness, exposure, and white-balance references. Keep them subordinate to the subject rather than distributing bright highlights across every surface.

These are optional production techniques, not universal style requirements. Do not add them to a flat, matte, printed, or intentionally degraded visual language when they conflict with the approved grammar.

## 7. Layered environmental density

A lived-in image does not require maximum object count. Organize density by depth and story function.

- **Foreground:** one or two large clean framing shapes, a contact surface, or the nearest active object.
- **Midground:** the active path, characters, props, and clustered story details that explain current use.
- **Background:** simple depth completion, location identity, and a small number of continuity landmarks.

Use traces of life such as one laundry line, smoke source, vegetable row, worn handle, half-used work surface, or weathered path instead of enumerating every possible object. Each detail should support geography, state, culture, action, or atmosphere.

Keep the movement lane and focal hierarchy readable. Background density may increase across a wide establishing shot and decrease across close coverage while preserving the same approved location.

## 8. Viewpoint and atmosphere

Different viewpoints expose different evidence of the same environment:

- external wide: weather direction, ground, practical light placement;
- external close-up: breath, moisture, reflected light, surface response;
- over-the-shoulder: foreground material response and facing-subject reaction;
- first person: visible sleeve or limb, breath, glare, hearing, balance;
- device or fixed observer: mount, shelter, lens, and microphone limits.

A viewpoint change does not reset weather, temperature, humidity, wetness, light, or sound.

## 9. Night scenes

Night remains legible through motivated sources such as moon, lamp, screen, fire, sign, vehicle light, window spill, or reflected street light.

Track source positions, readable surfaces, darkest permitted areas, reflective response, exposure across cuts, and the source that bridges the seam. Avoid uniform darkness, unrestricted blue grading, or unmotivated rim light.

## 10. Cultural, seasonal, and locale context

Approved world and locale rules may affect:

- dress, layers, footwear, and carried objects;
- architecture, practical lights, and materials;
- public distance, gesture, and contact;
- soundscape and language behavior;
- seasonal weather, vegetation, food, and routine.

Do not infer costume or behavior from a broad location label alone. Use approved evidence and character ownership.

## 11. Environment-driven appearance adaptation

Resolve a proposed adaptation from:

```text
environment facts
character physiology and body plan
owned wardrobe and inventory
approved locale and social rules
scene role and action needs
```

Possible changes include rolled sleeves, removed outerwear, gloves, umbrella, fan, wet fur, dusty clothing, or altered footwear. Record whether the proposal is scene-local, temporary, progressive, or persistent. It becomes canon only after approval.

## 12. Image quality follows the approved visual language

Do not append photorealistic quality language to an illustrated project. Preserve the approved line, shape, shading, surface, palette, edge, texture, and motion grammar.

Use clear focal hierarchy, coherent materials, controlled shadow and highlight placement, deliberate color balance, and readable contact. Intentional degradation must name a specific process and remain consistent.

## 13. Atmosphere and quality review

- sources, depth, and carriers are clear;
- weather affects something visible or audible;
- focal subjects and action remain readable;
- state persists correctly across shots;
- light source and landing stay coherent;
- material response is localized;
- viewpoint changes do not reset environment;
- sound beds and tails support continuity;
- cultural adaptation is approved and specific;
- quality follows the project style.
