# Third-Person Camera Direction

External character-aligned third person is the default camera family.

## 1. Camera geometry

Every external shot states:

- camera position relative to visible landmarks;
- height and angle;
- shot scale and lens behavior;
- focal character or objective scope;
- axis side and screen direction;
- start relation, path, speed, and landing for movement;
- expected occlusion;
- visible subjects and body regions.

Avoid abstract commands such as cinematic orbit. State the actual arc, distance, speed, subject relation, and landing composition.

A shot where the environment carries as much of the frame as the characters needs its perspective anchored as well:

- where the horizon sits in the frame, which fixes camera height;
- where lines converge, which fixes camera position along the space;
- what the near ground surface is, which fixes distance and scale.

Naming a location is not naming a viewpoint. A location word supplies a subject; convergence supplies a camera. When these anchors are absent the target invents a default vantage, and the invented one tends to be a chest-height frontal view of a generic version of that location. State convergence as camera-position language rather than as a drawing instruction: what recedes, toward which side, and how far away the eye level sits from the ground.

A plausible location also owes the audience its furniture. A prop that has no reason to stand where the shot puts it reads as an error even when it renders cleanly, so verify that the activity, the object it needs, and the place all belong together before the object enters the frame. When they do not, change the activity or change the place; do not keep the object and hope the frame hides the mismatch.

## 2. Axis of action

Declare the interaction axis when characters face, approach, fight, pass objects, or travel together.

Remain on one side unless the scene deliberately crosses the axis. A crossing needs one of:

- a continuous camera move that visibly passes the axis;
- a neutral-axis shot;
- a re-establishing wide shot;
- an explicit disorientation effect required by the story.

## 3. Screen direction

Track movement and facing across cuts. A character moving screen-right should not appear to reverse direction without a turn, axis crossing, or new geography.

## 4. Eyelines

Record eyeline height, direction, and target. Shot and reverse shot requires compatible eye levels and camera positions. A lower or higher eyeline may be deliberate when power or scale is part of the scene.

## 5. Movement vocabulary

External movement may include locked, pan, tilt, track, dolly, crane, arc, orbit, or handheld follow. Each move answers:

```text
Why does the camera move?
Where does it begin?
What path does it take?
How fast and how far?
How does it remain related to the subjects?
What may cross or occlude the lens?
Where does it land?
```

## 6. Character alignment

A character-aligned camera may show the focal character's face and body while respecting that character's knowledge. It does not reveal hidden objects or offscreen events unless the scene changes focalization.

## 7. Shot function

Choose a shot for geography, cause, action, reaction, contact, reveal, landing, or bridge. Do not create a master, medium, close-up, reverse, and insert automatically.

## 8. Shot scale and lens behavior

Shot scale is not only crop. It changes what continuity evidence is available.

- extreme wide: geography, scale, travel path, group relation;
- wide or full: stance, locomotion, silhouette, wardrobe, prop carriage;
- medium: body language, hand use, conversation blocking;
- medium close: face plus shoulders, hands or shared object when framed deliberately;
- close-up: expression, identity detail, reaction, small state reveal;
- insert: one prop, contact, wound, mechanism, or clue.

Describe lens behavior through visible consequences:

- wide perspective increases near-far size difference and spatial energy;
- moderate perspective keeps body and environment readable;
- compressed perspective flattens depth and isolates expression;
- close focus can transfer attention within one coordinate system.

Do not use a lens number as a substitute for composition unless the target or production process needs the actual number.

## 9. Camera height and power

Camera height affects relation and information:

- eye level supports parity and clear eyelines;
- lower camera increases physical presence and foreground foreshortening;
- higher camera exposes layout and can reduce apparent dominance;
- floor or table height can align with a child, animal, object, or contact event;
- shoulder height supports tracking and conversation coverage.

Use height to serve story and geography. Do not turn every authoritative character into an extreme low angle or every vulnerable character into a high angle.

## 10. External camera motivation

An external camera does not need a story-world operator, but its movement still needs an editorial purpose. Valid motives include:

- follow the dominant action;
- reveal a new spatial relation;
- preserve contact visibility;
- shift alignment from one character to another;
- lead the viewer toward a clue;
- create or release pressure;
- land on a continuation frame.

The camera may hold while characters move. A moving camera and moving subject should share a clear relationship. Competing paths make action harder to read and harder to continue.

## 11. Tracking and following

For a follow shot, specify:

```text
which subject leads
camera side and distance
relative speed
whether the frame leads or trails movement
what landmarks pass
how screen direction remains stable
where the camera stops
```

A track that begins beside a character and ends in front must describe the crossing and re-establish the axis. If the target cannot support the move reliably, split it into a stable follow and a motivated cut.

## 12. Arc and orbit

An arc or orbit is appropriate when changing visible relation around a mostly stable subject has story value. Record:

- center of the arc;
- radius or approximate distance;
- start and end angle;
- direction;
- speed;
- obstacles and occlusion;
- whether the move crosses the action axis;
- what new information becomes visible at the landing.

Avoid a full orbit used only as generic spectacle. It spends generation complexity, changes background geometry, and may expose unapproved state or identity surfaces.

## 13. Handheld external camera

Handheld is a physical motion grammar, not random shake. Define:

- operator relation to the subject;
- walking or stationary support;
- amplitude and frequency;
- reaction to impacts or turns;
- stabilization level;
- landing behavior.

Use handheld when immediacy, instability, pursuit, documentary relation, or a specific series style calls for it. Keep facial and contact readability protected.

## 14. Character-aligned focalization

The default external profile follows one focal character's attention and knowledge while keeping the character visible. Apply it through:

- shot order that follows what the character notices;
- selective depth or framing around their attention target;
- reaction shots placed after causes the character perceives;
- sound emphasis consistent with their location;
- concealment of facts they do not know unless a deliberate reveal changes scope;
- performance emphasis on their decisions and state.

The camera may temporarily leave the focal character for an insert or facing reaction. Record whether focalization changes or only coverage changes.

## 15. Objective and omniscient external shots

### Objective

Use for shared geography, procedures, group movement, or action where no single internal perspective controls information. Keep interpretation grounded in visible behavior and objective sound.

### Omniscient

Use only for a declared reveal or series grammar. Record what the audience learns, why the present characters do not, and how later scenes preserve that knowledge difference. Omniscient access is not a license for arbitrary cutaways.

## 16. External close contact

For hugs, handoffs, medical care, fights, or face contact:

- choose the side that keeps ownership readable;
- protect the key contact point from foreground occlusion;
- establish pre-contact positions;
- show physical response;
- define the end pose;
- preserve state and wardrobe deformation;
- use an insert only when it adds information not readable in the master.

A close external camera can show both participants' performance better than first-person. A first-person insert can add subjective pressure, but it should be motivated and continuity-linked.

## 17. External camera completion check

Before sealing an external shot:

- camera position resolves to visible landmarks;
- shot scale and lens behavior support the shot function;
- focalization and knowledge are explicit;
- axis side and screen direction are known;
- eyelines have target and height;
- movement has path, speed, subject relation, and landing;
- foreground objects and bodies have an occlusion plan;
- state and prop ownership remain visible or intentionally concealed;
- the landing can connect to the next shot.
