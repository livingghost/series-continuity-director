# Dialogue, Character Grammar, Audio, and Localization

Dialogue, sound, point of audition, music, subtitles, and localization are separate production layers. Preserve the exact line and its character function even when the selected picture-generation pass cannot carry it.

## 1. Dialogue ownership and character grammar

Every recurring character owns a language system in `character-profiles.md`:

- **Thinking grammar:** how the character organizes the world, such as verdicts, trades, commands, denials, questions, rituals, diagnoses, bargains, or gifts.
- **Vocabulary domain:** terms drawn from profession, origin, status, hobby, body plan, community, or worldview.
- **Sentence fingerprint:** habitual length, omissions, order, repairs, particles, interruptions, and rhythm.
- **Speech stage:** what vocabulary, grammar, register, or language ability is currently available at this story time.
- **Voice direction:** pitch range, breath, pace, texture, volume, and stable limits.

A catchphrase is optional. A way of thinking is mandatory.

Use two tests:

1. **Name-masking test:** hide the speaker label. The line should still sound attributable to the intended character.
2. **Function test:** if the line only explains visible room layout, repeats the action, or recites lore the scene already shows, give that job to image, action, or state and remove the line.

Verification exercise: have several characters express the same fact. Their logic, vocabulary, and sentence shape should differ.

## 2. Common synthetic-dialogue tells

Fix these on sight:

- complete explanatory sentences where a fragment, pause, or gesture would be natural;
- dialogue any character could say without changing wording;
- emotion stated directly instead of leaking through choice, avoidance, rhythm, repair, or action;
- customer-service politeness unrelated to the character or social context;
- symmetrical question-and-answer exchanges that feel mechanically balanced;
- stock exclamations and generic acknowledgements;
- translation shaped by another language's syntax rather than the spoken language;
- every visible speaker receiving a line merely because they are present;
- repeated restatement of names, relationships, objectives, or objects already established;
- lines that summarize hidden motives the focal character cannot know;
- identical sentence length and punctuation across the cast;
- dialogue that forces a complex hand action, relocation, and camera move into the same performance window.

A list is diagnostic, not a demand for slang or fragments. A formal or verbose character may use complete sentences when that is part of the approved grammar.

## 3. Conversation and revision rules

- Prefer action answers, counter-questions, partial answers, delayed answers, and sideways answers when character-true.
- A silent reaction is valid and may be stronger than a reply.
- Draft the full intended exchange, then cut at least one third of the words unless the character's established voice is deliberately verbose.
- Keep the exact speech stage. Do not unlock fluency, vocabulary, intimacy, or confidence early.
- Let interruption, hesitation, self-correction, unfinished lines, and breath carry character when appropriate.
- Preserve asymmetry. One character may ask while another acts, refuses, redirects, or remains silent.
- During important dialogue, keep body and camera movement small unless the movement is the line's one dominant action or a source performance carries it.
- Give listeners specific reactions instead of filling every pause with another line.
- Keep public mask, private behavior, current emotion, and long-term relationship distinct.

Cutting words must not erase the line's objective, speaker ownership, social register, or state. Concision is not neutralization.

## 4. Dialogue function

Each important line should perform at least one concrete job:

- pursue or resist an objective;
- change a relationship position;
- reveal or conceal information;
- force a decision;
- redirect action;
- complete a transfer, promise, refusal, warning, diagnosis, or invitation;
- expose a character-specific contradiction;
- create a sound bridge or performance hold;
- plant or pay off a recurring phrase.

Record the function in the director package. The exact line remains in the canonical dialogue language.

## 5. Dialogue density and stable visual window

Place important dialogue during a stable pose, a small linked action, or a designed performance-transfer window.

A useful window provides:

- a settled camera or one simple related movement;
- a readable face, deliberate profile, or intentional offscreen speaker;
- limited competing hand work;
- enough time for line length and breath;
- a chosen audio route;
- a reaction or landing after the line.

Possible routes include:

- same-pass native generation;
- separate speech generation;
- performance or lip-synchronization operation;
- audio post over a stable visual hold;
- offscreen dialogue;
- subtitle or caption track.

Preserve exact line, speaker, language, voice, timing, and performance even when the route changes. If a line competes with difficult movement, move it after landing, shorten it according to character grammar, split the shot, or route it to another pass.

## 6. Compose in the spoken language

Compose each line directly in the canonical spoken language and register. Do not write an English sentence first and mechanically translate it.

Preserve:

- subject omission;
- politeness or status level;
- particles and repair patterns;
- dialect and region;
- rhythm and sentence length;
- culturally natural indirectness;
- character-specific terminology;
- speech-stage limitations.

When a target has limited spoken-language support, keep the canonical line in project state and choose a separate audio, performance, or subtitle route. Do not silently change the character's language.

## 7. Point of audition

Point of audition is independent from camera ownership.

### Objective mix

Balanced scene audio with spatial direction and no privileged internal hearing.

### Character-subjective audition

May include breathing, pulse, ringing, reduced distant sound, altered clarity, or selective emphasis tied to one character's current state.

### Device or remote audio

Follows the declared microphone, mount, compression, obstruction, transmission path, distance, and recording limits.

A viewpoint cut does not automatically change the mix. Record the cause and transition when audition changes.

## 8. Functional sound design

Build the scene mix from:

```text
ambient bed
action and contact sounds
turn or reveal effect
dialogue and breath
music function
bridge or tail
```

Sound needs a source and production function. It may:

- cause an action;
- reveal space;
- carry a cut;
- confirm contact or material response;
- support performance;
- mask a seam;
- preserve continuity across clips.

Examples:

- rain on an awning causes a character to glance upward;
- pendant metal clicks against the workbench during a handoff;
- machinery masks a quiet line until the motor stops;
- a continuous room bed bridges an external shot to a first-person insert;
- a fixed camera microphone loses clarity when a door closes between it and the speaker.

Use the scoped wording rules in [`scoped-lexicon.md`](scoped-lexicon.md). Sound terms such as `muffled` or `faint` belong to a sound path, not to the whole visual grade.

## 9. Music

Record instrumentation, pulse, register, motif, authority, and edit function rather than relying only on a song title.

Music can:

- establish series, character, relationship, or location identity;
- build pressure;
- step aside for a functional turn effect;
- return after a decision;
- bridge scenes;
- track a chapter or long character arc;
- distinguish narrator-present from depicted-past time.

Plan music across the edited sequence when per-clip generation would restart it. Generated music is candidate material until accepted and mixed.

For narration or retrospective structure:

- keep only low sustained texture under dense narration and let melody answer in pauses;
- play the heaviest line dry, with no music, when that absence carries the intended weight;
- use an in-world metronome, such as knitting needles, sword practice, machinery, footsteps, or a fire, when a diegetic sound can keep time more naturally than a drum part;
- **Cross-episode motif arc:** let a recurring motif travel across episodes as complete, fragmented, off-key, varied, withheld, and finally complete when that progression tracks approved character or relationship state.

Do not force these techniques into every narration scene. Record the concrete cue, source, and edit point.

## 10. Subtitles and captions

Subtitles default to off. When requested, each track declares:

- language;
- exact text;
- delivery route: sidecar, metadata, burned-in, or another actual route;
- timing after picture is locked;
- translation style;
- speaker identification when needed;
- line breaks and reading constraints;
- authoritative source text.

Keep sidecar and metadata subtitles outside visual prompts. Burned-in text requires explicit intent and current target evidence.

## 11. Localization

Conversation language, director-package language, target-submitted language, each character's dialogue language, and subtitle languages are independent.

Translate from canonical dialogue, not from another translation. Preserve:

- character grammar;
- social register;
- names and glossary;
- intentional ambiguity;
- wordplay adaptation;
- speech stage;
- subtitle length and reading constraints.

Record deliberate adaptation, honorific loss, register change, or replacement wordplay instead of claiming literal equivalence.

## 12. Beat-template fatigue

Keep causal structure while rotating surface form. Review the previous two episodes or comparable scenes before finalizing a recurring pattern.

Possible openings:

- sound first;
- visible hand already mid-task;
- peripheral entrance;
- awakened by weight or contact;
- light opening through a gap;
- a held object already in dispute;
- continued action from the prior scene.

Possible reactions:

- listener close-up;
- held prop or interrupted task;
- posture change;
- offscreen line while watching the listener;
- silent refusal;
- delayed answer after contact or state settles.

Possible endings:

- object rather than face;
- character back or departing silhouette;
- dying sound;
- unfinished line;
- one body part or hand state;
- continuing action bridge;
- quiet relationship consequence;
- purposeful unresolved image.

Do not rotate forms mechanically. Change them when repeated sentence shape, coverage pattern, dialogue exchange, or landing would make adjacent episodes feel interchangeable.

## 13. Audio continuity and finishing

Across clips and shots, track:

- ambient bed and room-tone source;
- dialogue tail;
- effect decay;
- music phrase;
- point of audition;
- spatial direction;
- intended J-cut or L-cut;
- generated versus post-produced authority;
- final loudness target and destination.

Generated audio is candidate material. Review each track separately. Finish accepted audio before extracting an endpoint when sound affects the next cut.

A surface that produces audio alongside picture produces it per clip, which means it restarts at every
clip boundary and arrives at a different level in every take. That restart lands exactly on the seam
the edit is trying to hide, so treat generated audio as a source of foley and a scratch guide, never
as the programme's ambience. Measure the takes before assuming otherwise: a spread of more than a few
units of loudness across the clips of one scene is the level jump the audience will hear at every cut.

Lay the ambience instead as one unbroken region spanning every clip of the sequence, never one region
per clip and never re-triggered at a cut. The check is direct: mute the bed and replay two adjacent
shots. Muted, the cut should read as two different places; with the bed, as one place from two angles.
The bed buys nothing for state or identity errors, only for the seam.

Two practical constraints follow.

First, the bed has to come from somewhere. A video surface that generates sound tends to generate
events rather than sustained tone, so its output usually cannot be looped into a bed at all; check by
measuring the flattest few seconds available and looking at the spread. When there is no library, a
sound-design model asked for an explicitly unchanging environment is the cheapest source, and a
seamless-loop option where the surface offers one.

Second, do not run a dynamic loudness normaliser over the finished mix. It rides the gain, lifting the
quiet stretches and ducking the ones carrying foley, which reintroduces movement at the very cuts the
bed was laid to cover. Measure the programme once and apply a fixed gain.

## 14. Dialogue and audio completion check

- every important line has exact text, speaker, language, voice, and route;
- name-masking and function tests pass;
- synthetic-dialogue tells have been reviewed;
- current speech stage is preserved;
- dialogue length fits a stable visual window or has an alternate route;
- silence is used when stronger than another line;
- the previous two episodes were checked for repeated surface form when relevant;
- camera and audition relationships are deliberate;
- ambient bed and key effects have sources;
- music has a scene or sequence function;
- narration and motif techniques are intentional rather than automatic;
- subtitle policy is explicit;
- localization preserves character grammar and speech stage;
- audio bridges and tails support continuity;
- generated and post-produced authority is recorded;
- no ungenerated audio result is claimed.
