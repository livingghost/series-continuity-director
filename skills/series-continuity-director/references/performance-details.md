# Performance Detail Library

**Preparation and reuse:** Full applicable sources govern initial characterization and a changed scope. While preparing a scene, save actual definitions and applications using [Scene Persona](scene-persona.md). During writing or resumption in the reviewed scope, verify complete-source commitments and use that document; the instruction to read the full Persona applies to preparation, not to repeated execution within the reviewed scope. Reopen for new participants, topics, knowledge or portrayal intent, even when source hashes are unchanged.


Performance details serve the intended effect: they may change a reading, sustain it, conceal it, or leave an interval deliberately unresolved. Choose strength and duration from the full persona, applicable form, situation, counterpart, style, and viewing conditions, not from an emotion label or a universal restraint rule. See [Production Direction](production-direction.md) for the choice and observation record.

## 1. Four performance states

Keep these channels separate:

```text
felt state
expressed state
masked or performed state
physiological state
```

A character may feel fear, express calm, maintain a professional mask, and show shallow breathing.

## 2. Observable performance channels and ownership

Potential channels include:

- face, eyes, brows, mouth, muzzle, beak, or optic system;
- head timing and gaze;
- shoulders, spine, pelvis, stance, and weight;
- hands, paws, wings, tail, ears, crest, horns, or other approved anatomy;
- breath, voice, throat, and pacing;
- clothing, carried objects, and material response;
- light and environment response;
- distance, orientation, and contact behavior.

Use only approved anatomy and behavior.

Body-plan channels may include:

- humanlike: jaw, throat, hands, shoulders, posture, gaze, breath;
- long-muzzle canine: ears, muzzle, tail base and tip, jaw, nose contact;
- broad-muzzle feline: eyes, slow blink, whisker region, tail tip, shoulder roll;
- avian: crest, ruff, wing edge, beak orientation, feather compression;
- scaled or draconic: throat, wing membrane, tail, scale sheen, breath;
- equine or large herbivore: ears, neck, hoof, flank tension, head height;
- robot or machine: optic aperture, faceplate angle, joint speed, panel alignment, emissive response.

Character profiles define what is canonical.

## 3. Expression tiers

When amplitude is a meaningful choice, compare suitable forms of an approved signal. The examples below are a vocabulary, not three mandatory alternatives or a fixed body inventory.

### Small

- eye, brow, ear, crest, or optic adjustment;
- breath change;
- grip change;
- weight shift;
- small tail, wing, hand, or panel response.

### Medium

- posture change;
- one step;
- visible withdrawal or approach;
- moderate tail, wing, ruff, shoulder, or joint response;
- object placed down, raised, or repositioned.

### Large

- full-body release;
- defensive expansion;
- jump, charge, collapse, or transformation;
- major contact or relocation;
- approved signature-scale motion.

Select the strength that serves this depiction and remains readable in its context. Restraint, masking, exaggeration, and coordinated large actions are valid choices. Record the purpose of overlap rather than prohibiting it.

## 4. Performance under viewpoint and focalization

State projects differently by viewpoint.

### External character-aligned third person

- full posture, face, and interaction with space;
- social mask and private leakage;
- how other characters respond;
- visible state effects across the whole body.

### External objective third person

- visible behavior and objective sound only;
- no privileged internal interpretation;
- geography, action, and contact receive priority.

### Over-the-shoulder

- foreground tension in shoulder, hand, head, or held object;
- facing subject's expression and reaction;
- shared object, contact, and eyeline relationship.

### Embodied first person

- breath, balance, visible hands or limbs, gaze delay, sound, and contact;
- the camera owner's face remains unavailable unless reflected;
- internal state uses body and audition evidence the viewpoint can carry.

### Device or fixed observer

- performance remains visible only through the device's position, framing, audio path, and technical limitations;
- do not grant the device privileged access to hidden state.

## 5. Stillness

Stillness may communicate authority, grief, embarrassment, attention, tenderness, or danger. When the intended hold needs a visible or audible cue, consider the following examples. Absolute visual stillness or silence may instead be the selected effect:

- breath;
- cloth, hair, fur, feather, or panel movement;
- practical light change;
- weather or environment motion;
- another character's reaction;
- grip pressure;
- small eye, ear, tail, or optic movement;
- ambient sound with a visible cause.

Do not replace meaningful stillness with arbitrary camera motion.

## 6. Idle pose, signature move, and recurring behavior

A recurring character may have one approved idle pose that makes entrances or held shots recognizable and one signature move used sparingly enough to retain meaning.

Record:

```text
trigger
small, medium, or large expression tier
body support and weight
active anatomy
path and amplitude
material response
camera requirements
state and relationship conditions
effective story range
reference or performance assets required
```

The signature move is not mandatory in every episode. Repetition without function weakens it. A generated accident, unapproved flourish, or target artifact is not a signature and must not enter the character profile automatically.

## 7. Dialogue performance

For each important line, direct:

- objective and subtext;
- breath before, during, or after;
- gaze target;
- posture and hands;
- sentence shape, rhythm, and volume;
- interruption, hesitation, or repair;
- stable visual window;
- listener reaction.

Complex contact, relocation, and camera movement can compete with dialogue. Use a stable hold, source performance, or another audio route.

## 8. Relationship-specific performance

Relationship and context change performance. Record:

- public versus private posture;
- allowed distance and contact;
- who initiates approach;
- eye contact tolerance;
- object-sharing behavior;
- authority and obligation;
- current conflict, repair, trust, or concealment state;
- secrets each participant knows.

Relationship state is distinct from current emotion. Do not canonize one render gesture without approval.

## 9. Environmental performance

Environment changes behavior:

- cold tightens posture and changes breath;
- heat changes clothing, sweat, pace, and distance;
- humidity changes fur, hair, cloth, and fatigue;
- wind changes stance and loose materials;
- wet ground changes foot placement and speed;
- loud machinery changes speaking and attention;
- cultural and social setting changes public mask, clothing, gesture, and contact.

Use approved physiology and locale rules. Environment may propose, but not canonize, adaptation.

## 10. Performance review

- the action has a clear cause;
- performance belongs to the current state and relationship;
- signals use approved anatomy;
- felt, expressed, masked, and physiological states remain distinct;
- selected details perform different jobs;
- expression scale matches shot duration and complexity;
- the selected viewpoint can actually show or hear the signal;
- dialogue has a stable performance window;
- environment and materials respond consistently;
- final behavior forms a usable landing.

## 11. Material and light as performance

Bodies and materials can carry performance without adding a separate gesture.

- a shoulder line loosens gradually after tension;
- the base of a tail moves before the visible sweep;
- one ridge of fur, feather, hair, cloth, or panel lifts in alertness;
- wet material clings and changes silhouette, while drying restores volume;
- a sleeve, collar, mane, wing edge, horn, or casing catches a practical light source;
- a held object shifts pressure before a decision;
- a character blocks light from another subject, reading as protection or threat according to blocking and reaction;
- a silhouette gains facial or surface detail while approaching a light source;
- a flickering practical source reveals guarded performance in intervals;
- stepping out of shadow can mark an approved arc change when used sparingly.

Name light source, movement, and landing. Do not use lighting as a generic emotional label.

## 12. Choosing a performance from persona and intention

Keep the full applicable persona and its source available to the director. Decide what this occasion should convey or withhold, taking account of experience, habitual stance, the other participant, present conditions, and the intended treatment. The same named feeling need not produce the same expression. A held stance, a delayed reply, an exaggerated movement, or no outward response can each be appropriate.

At a material fork, compare concrete realizations and name the reason for the selected one. Do not generate alternatives for every small operation. Retain the source applicability and any intentional departure, its reason, scope, and what remains invariant in `direction`.

Describe the observable construction at the precision this task needs: participating structures from the declared subject, support, orientation, contact, timing, and the relation to the frame or sound. Several coordinated changes may be one intelligible action. A performance can also contain parallel actions and responsive stillness. Do not reduce this to one body part or impose a fixed distance and duration.

Check the chosen tool's actual inputs and returned evidence separately. A description a performer can follow is useful direction, not proof that a generation tool can realize it. An image fixes one instant; two consistent poses do not establish feasible motion between them. Use timed evidence when the unresolved question is speed, response, support transfer, or synchronization.

## 13. Coordinated details and simultaneous actions

Give each selected detail a job in the intended reading. Keep causal or expressive coordination explicit, and separate it from unrelated instructions competing for the same control. The number of details is not a universal limit. Inspect the whole result before deciding whether a gesture, sound, framing change, or held interval obscures or supports another.

A repair may require linked changes in timing, support, and framing. Record that set and the observation it addresses. Do not split a necessary coordinated action merely to satisfy a one-change rule.

## 14. Species and form vocabulary idea bank

This is a menu for character profiles, not a universal rule set. The approved body plan and current state decide what applies.

- **Human and humanlike forms:** jaw setting before a reply; knuckles whitening on a held object; a swallow moving the throat; heat rising at ears or neck; weight shifting between feet; a slow exhale timed with a decision; a sleeve pushed up or collar loosened as visible intent.
- **Long-muzzle canine forms:** tail-tip flick before a wider wag; ears folding forward before a snarl; a slightly open mouth with an eye crease as a smile substitute; nose-tip contact leaving one cool damp point; restrained mouthing that holds a wrist without biting.
- **Broad-muzzle feline forms:** slow blink as affection; head-bump with low purr; tail tip twitching before a lash; pupils rounding when pleased; a rough tongue over the hairline as an intimate family gesture.
- **Avian forms:** ruff or crest height tracking arousal; feathers slicked thin before threat; a quick beak-tip tap; beak-side nuzzle; careful preening of a companion's hair when the relationship supports it; a small hop the character tries to hide.
- **Scaled and draconic forms:** low throat hum felt as chest resonance; wing membranes half-spreading with excitement; scale sheen changing under state or light; slit pupils softening toward round; warm breath across hair or fur as gentle contact.
- **Equine and large herbivore forms:** ears stopping mid-swivel when fully absorbed; hoof stamp as punctuation; skin shivering along flank at light touch; neck height and head angle carrying caution or trust.
- **Robots and machines:** optic aperture changing before head rotation; servo speed slowing at hesitation; panel alignment tightening under authority; a manipulator pausing above contact; emissive intensity shifting with approved state; cooling vents, fans, or casing vibration carrying physiological equivalents.

Choose a signal and strength from the declared persona, form and purpose. Describe observable motion, held relation, or absence of a signal rather than using the species label as an instruction.

## 15. Large form-specific action in short segments

Select the number, amplitude, and overlap of actions from their purpose and the actual operation's supported controls. Preparation and recovery are useful when they make support, contact, or transformation readable; they are not mandatory phases for every depiction.

A discovered limitation belongs to the tested target, operation, form, duration, and evidence. It is not a rule for every short segment. When direct generation cannot carry a required sequence, consider timed references, key poses, separated elements, compositing, or editing. Preserve the necessary expression rather than automatically reducing it to a single small gesture.

### Reusing prepared scene Persona material

Use [Scene Persona material](scene-persona.md) to distinguish full-source preparation from scoped reuse.
On first preparation or materially changed scope, read the complete applicable Persona, intent, world and
state, and save the exact definitions together with the scene applications. A prepared material document
is not a short trait summary. For ordinary writing, local repair and resumption within that scope, verify
the complete source hashes and use the prepared document; do not require the full-template preparation
read again. Source changes, new topics or participants, knowledge changes and unsupported choices reopen
preparation. Structural validation cannot certify semantic completeness or the truth of a reading claim.
