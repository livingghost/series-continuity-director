# Physical Contact Across Viewpoints

Contact requires clear ownership, timing, occlusion, support, feedback, state, and landing. Viewpoint changes visibility, not the physical event.

## 1. Universal contact record

For important contact, record:

```text
initiating subject
moving limb or body region
target subject or object
side and approach path
contact point
occlusion order
support and balance
pressure, weight, temperature, and material response
emotional response
release or held end state
prop ownership before and after
```

Use this record across every viewpoint.

## 2. Viewpoint-specific staging

### External camera

Choose an angle that keeps ownership and support readable. Use close coverage only for a distinct contact detail. Match action and ownership across cuts.

### Over-the-shoulder

Keep foreground anatomy attached. Preserve facing-subject hands, expression, and contact without merging them into foreground forms.

### Embodied first person

Assign camera-owner limbs a destination and frame edge. State any contact occlusion, body-caused camera response, and final visibility.

### Device or fixed observer

Respect device position, field of view, mount, and occlusion. Internal records do not make hidden contact visible.

## 3. Handoffs and transfers

A visible transfer has ordered phases:

1. current owner presents the object;
2. receiving hand, paw, manipulator, or other approved anatomy approaches;
3. both share contact briefly;
4. receiving grip closes;
5. old owner releases;
6. object settles with the new owner.

Record object orientation, condition, and support. Canonize transfer only after acceptance. Offscreen transfer needs an approved atomic event and later visible ownership.

## 4. Hugs and body enclosure

Choose the contact type:

- frontal hug;
- side hug;
- over-the-shoulder hug;
- seated embrace;
- protective enclosure;
- brief greeting;
- restrained or reluctant contact.

Record arm ownership, height relation, head position, torso contact, pressure, cloth or fur response, and release. One participant may initially stiffen and then soften. Show the change through breath, shoulders, hands, ears, tail, wings, posture, or weight.

External cameras show both bodies; shoulder and first-person views emphasize response, pressure, occlusion, breath, hands, or balance.

## 5. Face and near-face contact

Lower-complexity options include:

- forehead contact;
- nose-tip contact;
- cheek contact;
- crown or hair contact;
- hand to cheek;
- brief muzzle or beak-side nuzzle;
- hand kiss.

Use approved anatomy. Record approach, point, duration, occlusion, pressure, and retreat. Protect sensitive anatomy, accessories, and camera position.

Prop-mediated near-face contact follows the same rules.

## 6. Carrying, lifting, and support

### One character carries another

Record anatomy-appropriate support. Separate grip establishment from lift, then show weight transfer, material response, and stable hold.

### Object lift

Record grip, mass, center of gravity, limb chain, release, path, and landing. Heavy props affect posture, contact, and pace.

### First-person lift

Name support points and let camera height follow the body. Keep one continuous embodied viewpoint.

## 7. Medical and care contact

Care scenes may include bandaging, inspection, support, feeding, washing, grooming, temperature checking, repair, or assisted movement. Record:

- consent or established relationship boundary when relevant;
- exposed body or material region;
- tool, medicine, cloth, or repair material;
- hand or limb ownership;
- pressure and sensitivity;
- state change;
- privacy and focalization;
- final wardrobe, bandage, equipment, or repaired state.

Do not reveal a concealed injury through an external angle unless the scene disclosure plan allows it.

## 8. Combat and forced contact

Combat requires a clear cause, action line, impact point, reaction, and recovery. Record:

- attacker and defender;
- active limb, weapon, or object;
- trajectory;
- block, dodge, restraint, or impact;
- contact ownership;
- body, clothing, and equipment response;
- ground or environment feedback;
- injury proposal;
- end positions and screen direction.

Do not combine unrelated attacks and camera moves. Injury remains proposed until accepted as a state event.

## 9. State, inventory, and canon

Contact may change:

- prop ownership or possession;
- injury or healing state;
- clothing condition or layer state;
- equipment attachment;
- relationship state;
- knowledge and disclosure;
- character position and support.

Keep planned events separate from contact direction. Canonize only after acceptance; multi-entity ownership changes remain atomic.

## 10. Contact risk and camera choice

On current video surfaces, natural single-body motion (talking, walking, gesturing) holds, while two-body physical interaction such as a handshake, hand-to-hand handoffs, and any shot that keeps hands large in frame for long are where consistency breaks. Keep such contact short, isolate it in its own clip, cover the hands at a scale where errors are small, or carry the contact in an accepted still and cut around it.

Lower geometric complexity:

- simple handoff;
- high-five;
- one-hand support;
- forehead or nose contact;
- side hug;
- stable seated touch.

Moderate complexity:

- carrying;
- close two-hand care;
- over-the-shoulder hug;
- shared tool use;
- face contact with partial occlusion.

Higher complexity:

- spinning while holding;
- walking in a tight embrace;
- several simultaneous contacts;
- intricate interlocked fingers;
- close facial collision during fast camera movement;
- multi-character pile or grapple.

These are editorial starting points, not universal model limits. Update target-scoped tactics only from preserved run evidence.

## 11. Contact continuity review

- limb, hand, paw, wing, or manipulator ownership stays stable;
- digit count and orientation remain readable where required;
- object ownership changes only at the intended phase;
- contact produces pressure, deformation, balance, movement, or sound;
- support and center of gravity remain plausible;
- occlusion is purposeful and viewpoint-consistent;
- state, wardrobe, and equipment respond correctly;
- reaction follows cause;
- the end pose is stable;
- subsequent shots use the accepted contact outcome.
