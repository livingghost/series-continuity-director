# First-Person and Device Camera Profiles

First-person is available per shot. It is not the suite default. Use it when embodied perception, direct interaction, restricted knowledge, or device evidence performs a concrete job that external coverage does not perform as well.

## 1. Embodied first-person ownership

The camera belongs to one character body.

- Camera height follows the owner's body and support.
- Gaze changes through eye, head, neck, torso, or whole-body movement.
- Visible hands, paws, wings, knees, feet, or other anatomy belong to the owner.
- Contact specifies which owner limb enters from which frame edge and where it lands.
- Looking into the lens means looking toward the camera owner.
- Breathing, balance, pain, fatigue, wetness, and equipment may affect image and audition.
- The owner's face is not externally visible in one continuous embodied shot unless a real reflection, display, or deliberate composite is present.

Before each visual clause, confirm:

```text
where the owner body is supported
which way the head and torso face
what the current angle can actually see
which owner limbs are available in frame
what remains outside the field of view
```

Do not reveal geometry behind the owner or outside the current angle without a motivated turn, reflection, display, edit, or viewpoint transition.

## 2. Functional owner presence

The owner's body appears through functional evidence, not constant self-description.

- In a short shot, use one or two meaningful hand, arm, cuff, knee, foot, balance, breath, or weight actions when the story and framing support them.
- Do not insert repeated sleeve grips, finger twitches, idle hand entries, or other filler merely to prove that a camera owner exists.
- Give the camera owner's voice the same tone, breath, language, and performance direction as any visible character.
- Show first-person emotion through breath, balance, hesitation, grip, gaze avoidance, a stopped action, or altered movement. Use inner monologue only when the approved series format includes narration.
- Follow the project's approved body-visibility rule. A project may hide the owner's face and full body, allow selected limbs, or deliberately use a reflection, display, or composite. Do not assume one policy for every series.
- Keep visible owner anatomy compatible with the current identity, state, wardrobe, equipment, and handedness.

A body cue earns its place when it performs action, reveals state, establishes scale, carries contact, motivates camera motion, or changes interpretation.

## 3. Gaze and attention techniques

Use one or two techniques only when they serve the scene, a difficult action, or a seam.

- **Gaze ellipsis:** the owner looks away for a motivated task and looks back after a difficult or low-value intermediate action is complete. The offscreen completion must remain physically and narratively plausible.
- **Blink cut:** one blink can mark a small time skip when the target or edit path supports a clean blackout and the skipped state change is acceptable.
- **Focus handoff:** a near object begins as the attention anchor; focus or gaze transfers to a subject already located in the same coordinate system.
- **Peripheral entrance:** a character enters from a known frame edge established by prior geography.
- **Held gaze:** the camera remains on one point while breath, cloth, light, weather, another character, or sound keeps the shot alive.
- **Gaze avoidance:** the owner looks toward hands, clothing, floor, shoes, or a held object when direct eye contact would contradict performance.
- **Sound-first turn:** a sound occurs from a known direction, the owner reacts, then the gaze turns and re-anchors on its source.

Do not repeat the same technique mechanically across consecutive shots or episodes. It should change attention, hide a specific production difficulty, reveal information, or support performance.

## 4. The body drives the camera

Permitted movement is body-caused:

- crouching lowers the view;
- standing or being lifted raises it;
- leaning changes height and distance together;
- walking creates a restrained step rhythm;
- a stumble tilts and recovers according to balance;
- pain or fatigue may shorten a step, delay a turn, or bias weight;
- an arm, blanket, shoulder, door, or held object can occlude the view for a transition;
- an over-the-shoulder external shot can replace a difficult close contact without pretending the first-person camera left the body.

Record trigger, body cause, amplitude, path, and re-anchor. Do not describe a free crane, dolly, orbit, or external track unless the camera belongs to a declared mechanism that can perform it.

Adjacent action phases must connect. The end body position and gaze of one beat are the starting body position and gaze of the next.

## 5. Stillness without a dead frame

A held breath may reduce normal sway for one beat. Do not request prolonged total frame stillness from a living body.

Keep one or two living cues:

- breath or pulse;
- weight settling;
- cloth, hair, fur, feather, or equipment movement;
- practical light change;
- weather or environment motion;
- another character's reaction;
- a functional sound with a visible response.

Stillness should express attention, authority, fear, tenderness, shock, or concealment. Do not replace meaningful stillness with arbitrary camera motion.

## 6. Optics and physiology

Use optical or physiological effects only when the owner state and approved visual language support them.

- waking blur that resolves once at the opening;
- dark adaptation after entering a dim space;
- a backlit silhouette becoming readable as the subject approaches or exposure settles;
- glare response from a named source;
- a tear-glazed focal effect when tears are part of the current state;
- tunnel vision under acute stress, used sparingly;
- brief heartbeat emphasis at one important instant;
- vision interrupted by a real eyelid, hand, cloth, spray, smoke, or object.

These effects must not erase the approved style or become a global softness filter. Record what causes the effect, how long it lasts, what remains readable, and how normal perception returns.

Reflection shots require a real reflective surface, a camera path that can see it, and a decision about whether the owner's identity is allowed to appear. A reflection does not automatically authorize an external body view.

## 7. Held or worn device profile

A device camera is not an eye. Define:

```text
device type and operator
grip or mount
field of view and optical behavior
focus system
stabilization
available zoom or lens change
capture faults permitted by the device and era
audio source and compression
visible operator limbs, device frame, strap, or mount
```

Possible capture behaviors include:

- autofocus searching between the subject and a nearer object before it settles;
- framing slightly off-center because the operator follows a subject who moved first;
- the device arriving late so a turn has almost finished when the frame catches up;
- a zoom overshooting and correcting back when the device actually has zoom control;
- walking shake and corresponding motion blur;
- a finger, strap, casing, visor edge, or mount crossing one corner briefly;
- focus breathing while a lens or focus system fails to lock and then settles;
- exposure stepping when moving between very different light levels;
- rolling-shutter skew only when the chosen device and movement plausibly support it;
- microphone handling noise, wind overload, clipping, or transmission loss tied to the declared audio path.

Use at most one deliberate capture fault per beat. Several faults in one shot read as a damaged file and compete with the action the shot exists to show.

An eye does not hunt for autofocus, carry a strap, overshoot a zoom, or show a device casing. A fixed security camera does not inherit body sway. A body camera does not zoom unless the actual device supports it. A phone with continuous autofocus behaves differently from a manual lens. Choose faults from the declared mechanism, not from a generic found-footage style string.

## 8. Fixed diegetic observer

A security camera, room monitor, dashboard camera, inspection camera, vehicle sensor, or machine optic has:

- fixed or mechanically limited mount;
- field of view;
- lens and distortion;
- obstruction and housing;
- recording or access history;
- time display or overlay policy;
- audio source;
- permitted pan, tilt, zoom, or tracking behavior;
- known blind zones.

Mechanical movement is valid only when the declared device supports it. A fixed observer can cut to another fixed observer, but the transition must identify a new camera source rather than pretending one camera teleported.

## 9. Time control

First-person and device shots can carry time through concrete visual or editorial devices:

- **Object-anchored time skip:** one object remains in a stable screen relation while environment, light, wardrobe, or state changes around it.
- **Same-composition repeat:** a later shot repeats a prior composition so relationship, state, or environment change becomes visible through difference.
- **Blink or occlusion skip:** a brief full-frame obstruction bridges a small elapsed interval when the before and after states remain understandable.
- **Heartbeat emphasis:** one short subjective emphasis marks an acute decision, impact, or fear response.
- **Short repeated compositions:** a controlled series of repeated frames can carry comedy, routine, or procedural progression when the edit supports clean cuts.

A time-control device does not authorize arbitrary state change. Resolve the new story time and approved events before generating the later image.

## 10. Sound-driven camera and point of audition

Sound can motivate gaze without forcing a camera cut:

- sound occurs from a known direction;
- owner body reacts before or during the turn;
- gaze follows the source;
- frame re-anchors on a visible landmark or subject.

Point of audition may remain objective, become character-subjective, or follow a declared device. A cut does not automatically change hearing. Record breathing, ringing, obstruction, microphone direction, ambient bed, and the moment normal hearing returns.

## 11. Direct interaction with the camera owner

Looking into the lens is eye contact with the owner. A hand, paw, wing, cloth, tool, or prop may enter from a known edge to offer, block, cover, tap, steady, examine, or protect.

For direct interaction, record:

```text
approaching character or object
owner body position
entry edge
contact point
occlusion order
physical camera or body response
sound response
release or held end state
```

Avoid several unrelated touches in one beat. Contact with the lens or device must produce a plausible camera response based on the owner body or mount.

## 12. External visibility and viewpoint transitions

A continuous embodied shot cannot show the owner externally without a valid mechanism. To show the owner's face, posture, injury, or reaction, use:

- a real mirror or display already inside the shot;
- a fixed in-world camera feed;
- an explicit external reaction shot;
- a designed split-screen or composite;
- another character's viewpoint;
- an accepted edit between profiles.

A transition into or out of first person records camera ownership change, focalization, audition, prop ownership, state visibility, action phase, and bridge. The external reaction begins from the accepted end state of the first-person shot, not from a planned pose.

## 13. Starting camera-motion risk heuristics

These are soft editorial defaults, not universal model limits.

### Lower risk

- gaze ellipsis;
- object entering from a known edge;
- sound-first turn;
- simple occlusion;
- held gaze with living environment;
- backlit approach;
- one controlled step or lean.

### Moderate risk

- blink cut;
- frame-within-frame transition;
- object-anchored time skip;
- controlled focus handoff;
- body-height change with simple contact;
- short device autofocus search;
- view touched by one object or hand.

### Higher risk

- fast whip;
- complex reflection;
- tackle or body flip;
- rapid focus changes;
- several device faults in one beat;
- simultaneous height change and face contact;
- owner carried while manipulating a prop;
- fast movement through several occlusions.

Update these heuristics only from preserved runs in `production-state.md`, and scope each update to the actual target, operation, device, and shot family.

## 14. First-person completion check

- camera owner or device is explicit;
- owner body support and visible limbs are coherent;
- every gaze change has a body or optical cause;
- visible information fits the current field of view;
- camera motion is physically available to the owner or device;
- optical and capture effects match owner physiology or device mechanics;
- no more than one deliberate device fault competes with a beat;
- direct interaction has ownership, contact, response, and landing;
- time skips resolve story state rather than inventing it;
- point of audition is deliberate;
- transitions preserve state, prop ownership, action phase, and accepted geometry.
